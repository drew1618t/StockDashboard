# WPR's company views before the September 13 changes

September 6, 2026 · *WPR's growth stocks + new stock ideas* · Extended portfolio review

[Source video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xAvMFbo9OlI) · [Formatted transcript](transcript.md) · [Exact evidence](evidence.md) · [Structured company views](company_views.json) · [Later September 13 report](../2026-09-13_gj2AlI4J-6Q/report.md)

WPR is reducing exposure to companies whose immediate results do not support their longer-term promises. He has sold Bloom Energy and IREN, cut Credo substantially, and redistributed capital into other holdings. Yet he explicitly says these decisions are uncertain: his usual response is to trim or sell when the coming quarter does not look likely to outperform, then consider re-entering after a strong report, even at a higher price. [E03] [E04] [E05] [E06]

This video also captures a research correction. He initially liked Semtech's planned three-to-fourfold fab-capacity expansion, then learned it applied to a small acquired photonics operation. He lowers his estimate of its company-wide revenue significance and considers reducing the position. That is a possible action, not a completed trim. [E13] [E14]

This is an earlier opinion snapshot. September 13 remains the latest supplied reference for current comparisons. ## Portfolio structure and capital transfers

The four high-confidence holdings are Astera Labs at 17.6%, Figure Technology at 16.3%, Silicon Motion at 16.1%, and Ethos Technologies at 14.5%. They total 64.5%. Lumentum is 6%, Onto Innovation 5.8%, and Micron 5.8%. Adding the smaller holdings above and 2.2% unnamed foreign-listed technology produces a rounded disclosed total of 99.8%. The top ten total 90.5%. WPR reports a 27% year-to-date return; this is his statement, not a reconstructed performance calculation. [E02; allocation slide](evidence.md#E02)

He wants more diversification because AI-hardware holdings dominate after sales of other businesses. He will not add a non-AI company merely to diversify: it still needs to meet his standards. This is a stated preference, not evidence that he has a fixed sector limit. [E02]

He presents the week's transactions chronologically:

1. Sells Bloom, representing 1% of portfolio value, to start Semtech because he sees better near-term upside. [E03]
2. Sells IREN, representing 2.7%, and reallocates across Onto Innovation, Lumentum, Red Violet, SanDisk, and Figure. The individual destination percentages are described as being on the video slide, but are not spoken in the captions; they are not reconstructed here. [E03]
3. Sells 5.1% of portfolio value from Credo and reallocates into Onto Innovation, Figure, Micron, Astera Labs, and Silicon Motion. He calls this Credo's second consecutive underdelivering report. [E03]
4. Transfers 0.6% of portfolio value in cash to his international brokerage and distributes it roughly evenly among the names captioned “Fison,” Macronix, and SK Hynix. The first name likely refers to Phison, but that spelling is not verified from the slide or audio here. [E04]
5. Sells another 2% of portfolio value from Credo and buys Semtech, Kulicke & Soffa, and Red Violet. [E04]

The two Credo reductions sum to 7.1% of portfolio value, not 7.1% of the Credo position. The remaining disclosed weight is 1.7%. These transaction descriptions are not enough to infer execution prices, exact share counts, or each destination's purchased weight. [E02] [E03] [E04]

## His explicit approach to weak guidance and future promises

WPR describes a difficult situation: an acceptable report and weak next-quarter guidance, paired with optimistic commentary or annual guidance. He says the correct response is often unclear and acknowledges that respected investors can disagree widely. His typical choice is to trim or sell if the upcoming quarter lacks a strong chance of outperforming and better opportunities are available. [E05] [E06]

He recognizes the counterargument of holding while the longer-term thesis remains intact. His own preference is to wait for an impressive report that confirms growth and then re-add, even at a higher price. He also warns himself against treating an immediate price increase after a sale as proof that the decision was wrong. The video includes his reaction to IREN rising after he sold; it is part of his stated decision discipline, not this report's outcome assessment. [E06]

This is stronger evidence of his process than an inferred rule derived only from trade history. It is still a typical response, not an unconditional instruction to sell any company with a weak quarter.

## Existing holdings: what his research changed

### SanDisk

He sees sustained NAND demand, longer customer commitments, and a more strategic customer relationship. He discusses new memory products and high-bandwidth flash, and recounts a large joint investment with Kioxia. The concern is the supply chain: DRAM procurement in Taiwan, shared fabs in Japan, and assembly in China. He explicitly calls this his top concern. It predates the September 13 trim and should not be treated as a rationale invented afterward. [E07] [E08]

### Cohu

WPR likes that 53% of sales are recurring, while noting that software ARR itself is only $1.2 million. Do not equate the whole recurring component with software subscriptions. He reviews high-bandwidth-memory inspection systems and customer qualifications, then highlights silicon-photonics testing and co-packaged-optics opportunities. [E09] [E10]

The comparison with expectations matters most to him: $170 million of next-quarter revenue guidance, described as 35% annual and 14% sequential growth, versus prior analyst expectations that had no quarter above $160 million over the coming year. He also recounts a full-year growth-guide increase from 25% to 35%. His attraction is evidence of the company exceeding the market's prior model. [E10]

### Kulicke & Soffa

He reports two strong quarters, with 123% annual growth in the latest one. The leadership transition complicates his view: he questions the incoming CEO's prior record at Enovix but values the executive's Micron experience. He cites a company announcement about thermal-compression bonding for co-packaged optics. [E11] [E12]

He thinks the stock is substantially undervalued after the report and relative to Besi, which he describes as having similar revenue but roughly three times the valuation. This is WPR's comparison; matching revenue alone does not establish equivalent margins, product exposure, or growth quality. [E12]

### Semtech

His reported growth sequence is 16%, then 33%, then next-quarter guidance for 54% annually and 20% sequentially. He describes strength across copper, fiber, and photonics, and says he initially liked the new fab and its ramp. [E04] [E13]

Follow-up research changes the interpretation. The fab came through an acquisition, starts from a small base, and affects the photonics operation rather than the entire company. Tripling or quadrupling that fab's capacity would not triple or quadruple total revenue. He says he may reduce his position slightly. This shows an actual revision in his understanding, but no completed reduction is established by the video. [E14]

### Red Violet

The August 27 Midwest Ideas conference raises his confidence. He reviews collections, law enforcement, financial/corporate risk, emerging markets, and real-estate uses. He recounts a large law-enforcement customer win, an expanding government pipeline, and movement toward larger customers. [E15]

The economics attract him: a usage-based API, customers that can generate more activity as they grow, and a largely fixed cost structure. He is surprised that seven of the top ten identity providers are customers. Those customer and market claims remain management statements as recounted by WPR. His own conclusion is explicit: the additional information increased his confidence. [E15]

### Richardson Electronics

WPR uses another conference presentation to understand the business. He describes semiconductor-related exposure within its largest division, with Lam Research and MKS as major customers, plus legacy products, microwave technologies, wind-energy products, and a medical business. He recounts $32 million of cash, no debt, and quarterly net income of $3.7 million versus $1.1 million a year earlier. [E16] [E17]

This section supports a positive research update for a 0.9% starter. It does not contain an explicit new purchase or a promotion to high conviction. The captioned assertion that a solution extends the life of “wind turbines” from 12–18 months to ten years is ambiguous about the component involved; it should not be reused as an established turbine-life claim. [E02] [E17]

### SiTime

He likes the timing technology, applications such as satellites, and the Renesas timing acquisition. He describes the acquisition as margin-accretive, complementary, and a good deal that removes a competitor. His reported organic growth is 127%, with data-center growth of 181%; next-quarter organic revenue guidance is $200 million to $210 million. [E18] [E19]

The tiny 0.5% weight reflects two stated concerns: expense and integration risk. He acknowledges management saying integration is ahead of schedule but wants to see how the combined business is absorbed. These concerns already exist a week before the disclosed sale. [E19]

## The positions he is leaving or reconsidering

### Credo: the next guidance matters more than the next reported growth rate

WPR separates two comparisons. The upcoming reported quarter faces a $268 million prior-year base; revenue above $535 million would still exceed 100% annual growth. The quarter that will then be guided faces a $407 million base. He calculates that a guide of $639 million would imply about 57% annual growth, while even $700 million would imply about 71%. These are scenarios discussed by WPR, not guidance that Credo has already issued. [E20]

He worries the market will react poorly after becoming accustomed to triple-digit annual growth. He also compares Credo's flattening and hoped-for reacceleration with other semiconductor holdings that are still ramping. His stated conclusion remains positive on the long-term business, but he may sit out the next quarter. As of this video he has trimmed, not fully exited. [E02] [E20]

### Bloom Energy: sold while still weighing the merits

He likes the fuel-cell product, apparent limited direct competition, earnings beats, and improving gross margin. His objections include a stated 17× run-rate sales valuation, the much larger market capitalization implied by multiplying the share price, roughly 33% gross margin, potential scandium sourcing exposure, seasonality, and past dilution. These are his concerns and questions, not independently established supply failures or forecasts of future dilution. [E21]

He both discloses a sale and says he is on the fence about keeping an allocation. Preserve that nuance: the completed action is the sale, while his opinion remains mixed. [E03] [E21]

### IREN: confidence in management breaks

He says the CEO's post-earnings comments on X were the final trigger. He objects to additional messaging about the ramp coming in 2027, the presentation of revenue in the release, no separately listed R&D expense, a sports partnership, and executive compensation. These are WPR's interpretations of management behavior and disclosures. In particular, not seeing a separate R&D line does not establish that a company does no research. [E22]

The documented action is a sale of the position, with proceeds spread across existing holdings. His comments about the subsequent rally do not change that dated decision. [E03] [E22]

Sources: [E23] [E25] [E26] [E27] [E28]. These are rounded figures in the video. Their accounting bases and valuation denominators have not been independently reconciled.

Microchip reaches his attention through SiTime competitor research. He sees growing data-center exposure and strong cash generation, but worries about the scale of the business, supply constraints, and debt. He initially downplays debt risk because the interest rate is fixed, yet still cites the weak balance sheet in his final pass. Preserve both statements rather than smoothing them into a uniformly positive or negative view. [E18] [E23] [E24] [E25]

He also calls the CFO speaking before the CEO a light yellow flag, not a major concern, and wants clearer numbers for the high-margin licensing operation. These are his presentation and disclosure preferences, not evidence of misconduct. [E24]

Diodes has slower reported growth and lower gross margin, but he likes its improving outlook, profitability, smaller size, and lower valuation. He contrasts the current 2.1× sales multiple with 3× when he reviewed it in May, attributing the improvement to a lower share price and higher revenue. He recounts cash generation and automotive/computing growth, then says he is considering a tiny starter for likely growth above 30% and increasing data-center power complexity. No purchase is disclosed. [E26] [E27] [E28]

The comparison suggests that faster current growth and higher gross margin alone do not determine his choice. This is this report's synthesis of the two decisions, not a stated universal rule.

## Use in future comparisons

This video adds several specific questions to the comparison process: is guidance strong enough to support the near-term thesis; how does it compare with prior market expectations; how much of a segment's capacity expansion reaches total revenue; and what did follow-up research actually change? His examples are Credo, Cohu, Semtech, and Red Violet respectively. [E20] [E10] [E14] [E15]

His closing process is to remain flexible, find companies with recent strong reports, use technology conferences to deepen understanding of recent additions, and follow with 10-K research. These are stated research steps. They do not establish that a 10-K review is always completed before an initial position. [E29]

For each company, use the latest dated explicit view available, while retaining this report to explain how that view evolved. September 13 is newer overall, but it does not replace September 6's detailed reasoning for companies it barely discusses. Keep allocation changes, disclosed trades, intentions, and interpretations separate.
