# WPR's strategy through Philip Fisher

September 17, 2026 · *Legendary growth investor Philip Fisher's strategy*

[Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N_MDoqO79Z0) · [Formatted transcript](transcript.md) · [Exact supporting passages](evidence.md) · [Reusable strategy observations](strategy_views.json)

This is a strategy update, not a new holdings report. WPR explains how Philip Fisher's writings shaped his evaluation of hardware businesses, R&D, sales, management, margins, and financing. He also states where his own approach differs or where he finds an assessment difficult. No new purchases, sales, or allocations are disclosed in the reviewed transcript. September 13 remains the latest supplied portfolio snapshot.

The source has three distinct layers: WPR's account of Fisher's writings, WPR's explicit applications to his own strategy, and this report's suggestions for company comparisons. Fisher's original text and the historical examples have not been independently checked. This report therefore attributes the framework to Fisher **as WPR presents it**, rather than certifying quotations or treating every point as a rule WPR has adopted.

## What is most useful for our comparisons

WPR's direct statements give us several useful additions to the comparison process:

- He is considering greater emphasis on earnings alongside sales growth after contrasting Fisher's focus on per-share earnings with Saul's stronger emphasis on revenue. This is a reflection, not a declared numerical change in his screening criteria. [E02]
- He wants innovation that reaches customers: frequent related products, capable research, effective sales, and cost-efficient production. A strong product alone is insufficient. [E03] [E04] [E05] [E19] [E20]
- Lower margins may be acceptable when deliberately funding future growth, but he wants evidence that this is the cause rather than an unexplained decline. [E06]
- He values clear management communication and a strong CEO-CFO partnership. He admits difficulty assessing employee relations and integrity from public information. [E08] [E09] [E16]
- He may accept one yellow flag, or perhaps two light ones, when the rest of a business is outstanding. He does not define a mechanical count, weighting system, or override for every concern. [E14]
- His research starts with current business evidence and his own judgment; AI tools and investor communities provide additional inputs. [E15] [E17] [E21]

## Why Fisher mattered to him

WPR says he discovered Fisher's writings in 2023 after the software-stock downturn, when faster-growing hardware companies presented unfamiliar questions about capex and factory expansion. He found the discussion of research, sales, production, and leadership useful for those questions. His account of earlier semiconductor booms is part of his historical interpretation; the report does not independently endorse the claimed dates, forecasts, or analogies. [E01]

He describes a shared emphasis on a small number of outstanding companies, strong sales and earnings growth, and company research rather than macro trading. The 10–12-stock portfolio he discusses is Fisher's ideal in his account, not a newly announced target for WPR's own number of holdings. [E02]

## Research: current evidence, independent judgment, and outside inputs

WPR describes a sequence that can guide how we organize company work. He first checks cash and debt for an overview, then carefully reads the latest earnings transcript and sometimes a 10-K. He reviews financial metrics over the past year, including revenue, EBITDA, net income, and gross margin, and may look further back to see whether the business previously peaked and declined. Product roadmaps and unusual costs also deserve attention. [E17]

He cautions against spending hours on old reports or detailed historical balance sheets that do not help explain the current business. This is a preference for relevance, not a reason for this project to discard dated evidence: older sources remain necessary to reconstruct what he knew and when his view changed. The latter is this report's methodological distinction. [E17]

He likes to form his own view before asking other investors. He uses AI tools for questions about customers, competitors, and supply chains, and values discussions on Saul's board and the growth-stock-investing subreddit for ideas and criticism. He is much more cautious about crowdsourcing macro forecasts. [E17] [E21]

WPR compares his transcript reading and AI follow-up with Fisher's “scuttlebutt,” which he describes as conversations with customers, suppliers, competitors, and other informed people. This is WPR's analogy. An earnings call or an AI answer is not independently gathered stakeholder testimony. For our research, the practical application is to trace such claims to evidence rather than treating the research channel as verification. [E15]

## Innovation must become profitable production

WPR wants repeated product development, not dependence on a single existing line. He also recounts Fisher's warning that development moves through stages and that a pilot operation can be far removed from commercial-scale production. Delays and unexpected costs can occur during that transition. [E19]

He uses Astera's Scorpio announcement as an example of a product expanding perceived opportunity and surprising the market. That is his interpretation of the announcement and price reaction. It does not establish future revenue, successful commercialization, or a new purchase. In the same discussion, he acknowledges the danger of a stock rising on a product that remains far from meaningful sales. [E19]

Sales execution receives equal weight. His historical Cloudflare/Zscaler comparison illustrates his view that a strong product can underperform commercially if distribution, enterprise access, or the sales organization lags. These are retrospective judgments, not current recommendations on either stock. [E18]

He connects better production technology with lower costs, better margins, and sales competitiveness. He explicitly wants to avoid mistaking management's investor pitch for evidence that a product can be manufactured efficiently and profitably. [E20]

## Margin quality and financing deserve separate attention

WPR accepts the possibility that a company temporarily depresses profit by investing in research or sales. But he wants to verify the cause. “Investing for growth” should therefore remain a hypothesis until spending, products, customer demand, and economics support it. This application follows his stated caution; it is not a new quantified hurdle. [E06]

He recounts a warning about industry margins rising through repeated price increases and wonders how Fisher would evaluate the current memory boom and Micron. He does not answer that question with a trade or a changed allocation. The useful comparison is between pricing benefits, production improvements, and durable customer demand, rather than treating all margin expansion as equivalent. [E07]

On financing, WPR describes borrowing and dilution as related ways to raise capital, while acknowledging that amounts and terms matter. Debt and equity are not economically interchangeable; the report preserves this as his broad analogy rather than an accounting conclusion. He wants clear objectives and questions whether capital raises at Nebius and IREN are prudent, without announcing a new position change. [E12]

## Management assessment has practical limits

Clear, frank communication matters to WPR. Limited access to transcripts or useful information makes him less likely to invest. A CEO-CFO partnership is a positive signal to him, while dependence on one person is a concern. [E09] [E16]

He nevertheless says he places less emphasis than Fisher on evaluating integrity because it is difficult without meeting executives. He likewise struggles to judge employee relations in practice. These admissions matter: we should not assign confident management-quality scores from a polished call or a few public comments alone. That is this report's application of his expressed limits. [E08] [E16]

His examples involving ELF, Super Micro, and an ambiguously captioned battery company are illustrations of his thinking. They do not independently establish misconduct, current accounting problems, or the present quality of those businesses. [E10] [E11] [E13]

## Holding, selling, and valuation

WPR recounts Fisher favoring long ownership of exceptional businesses, avoiding macro-driven exits, and resisting the urge to sell solely because a stock has risen. He interprets one warning about selling an overpriced winner as referring to a full exit rather than a trim. That distinction is his interpretation, not an independently checked reading of the book. [E23] [E25]

He also recounts recognizing mistakes early and using capital elsewhere, and adds that modern business feedback is faster. The video does not resolve every tension between patient ownership and WPR's recent reallocations. It should not be used to overwrite the explicitly disclosed Credo and IREN exits in earlier videos or to invent an exception explaining every trade. [E26]

He prefers growth at a reasonable or cheap valuation, but does not treat a low multiple as sufficient. He discusses why a higher multiple may be justified by changed product potential, why a past share price is a poor standalone anchor, and why waiting for a tiny price concession can miss an opportunity. These are strategy principles, not advice to buy any named stock at its current price. [E12] [E23]
